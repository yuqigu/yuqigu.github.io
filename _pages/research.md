I study how to learn interpretable **latent structure** from complex data: what data can determine about hidden structure, how that structure can be estimated with scalable computation and reliable uncertainty, and how it can support measurement of people, scientific constructs, and AI systems. These questions recur across three connected research directions: (a) identifiability, deep latent variable models, and causal representations; (b) high-dimensional statistical estimation and inference for latent structure; and (c) statistical measurement for humans and AI systems.

The complete chronological record is on the [Publications]({{ '/publications/' | relative_url }}) page.

## 1. Identifiability, deep latent variable models, and causal representations

I start by asking what the data can determine, because interpretation depends on the answer. We show that useful quantities can sometimes be identified even when the full structure cannot. The conditions can run against intuition: I prove that dependence among latent attributes, often treated as a complication, can supply the identifying information that a sparse measurement design lacks.

Identification theory also guides how we design models. In Bayesian Pyramids and Deep Discrete Encoders, we build multilayer discrete latent architectures whose identifiability conditions inform their shape. I use <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/psy.2025.10078">tensor-unfolding arguments</a> to turn identification proofs into procedures for learning measurement structure. In causal representation learning, we separate three questions that are easily conflated: how latent variables are measured, how they are jointly distributed, and which causal relations among them observational data can distinguish.

## 2. High-dimensional statistical estimation and inference for latent structure

Different latent models leave different geometric signatures in the singular subspace of a high-dimensional data matrix. We use that geometry to turn hard recovery problems into scalable procedures: signal geometry encodes the latent configuration, and noise geometry governs how well configurations can be told apart. We build methods around four such geometries and apply them to data from political voting and surveys, population genetics, single-cell sequencing, education, and ecology.

Beyond these geometries, we tackle nonlinear models, where simple low-rank structure is lost. For generalized latent factor models with missing data, we combine a spectral initialization, a refinement step, and gradient descent with implicit regularization to obtain individual and simultaneous confidence statements without an additional debiasing step. We also study <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07167">likelihood refinement after spectral clustering</a>, <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29631">latent dependence beyond local independence</a>, and <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21263">transfer across related populations</a>.

## 3. Statistical measurement for humans and AI systems

Psychometrics is the intellectual foundation of this direction. I develop latent-variable models and theory for measuring attributes, response processes, and heterogeneous populations in educational, psychological, and biomedical settings, including cognitive diagnosis and clinical heterogeneity in Parkinson’s disease. I bring the same discipline to AI evaluation, where one aggregate score can hide both what is being measured and how certain the comparison is.

The two rows are complementary studies rather than one joint model. In the generalized latent factor analysis, we use answers alone to separate capabilities and quantify rank uncertainty; that paper is listed in Section 2. In LaRT, we add a process signal to answers. For a third route, we use question text in text-embedding-informed cognitive diagnosis to learn question–skill relationships and discrete mastery profiles.
